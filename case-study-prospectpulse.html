The Problem

Most small nonprofits don't have a development team. Sponsor and funder research is the first thing that gets skipped, and outreach ends up going to the same few names, or to no one.

Knowing who to ask

Which local companies and funders actually care about your mission, and which ones are just the biggest names in town?

Knowing how to reach them

Finding the right person, and the right way in, takes more digging than most staff have time for.

Trusting what you find

A list with an invented contact or an out-of-date deadline is worse than no list. You'll act on it, and it costs you credibility with a funder.

Accuracy: The AI Proposes, Code Decides

For every contact and grant, the AI has to give a link and a word-for-word quote from the page. Then ordinary code re-opens that page, confirms the quote is really there and really supports the claim, and only then marks the item Verified. The AI never gets to label its own work.

LabelWhat it means
Verified Found on the company's own website or in an official filing (a Form 990 counts only if dated within the last 12 months), with a source link, a quote re-checked by code, and checked within the last 90 days.
Verified general inbox No person is named, but a general giving address is printed on the company's own page. It's labeled as an inbox, never as a person.
On LinkedIn A person found on LinkedIn in a title that fits giving or community work. Not Verified: the tool never opens LinkedIn, so code can't check it.
Unverified Anything else, shown with where it was found. A name with no source is never shown.
Title only No name could be confirmed, so the report tells you which title to ask for.
Many companies don't publish a giving contact. When that's the case, the report says so instead of guessing. You always know what's confirmed and what still needs a phone call.

How It Works

1 · You share the basics

Your organization, mission, website, and the region you serve.

2 · Candidates are found from three angles

Corporate foundations, large local employers, and family and community foundations. Companies already delivered to you are never repeated.

3 · Each prospect is researched and checked

Every contact and grant is checked against its source by code. Fit scores are computed from four dimensions, never taken from the AI.

4 · I review it before it reaches you

I go through the draft myself, correct or confirm contacts, and only then deliver it.

Where It Started

ProspectPulse began as a single-company lookup: type a company name, get a sourced, honestly-scored fit brief. I tested it on three major Omaha corporations, using a youth-mentoring nonprofit seeking a corporate sponsorship as the seller. It didn't just return data. It returned calibrated, honest judgment.

CompanyFitWhat the Tool Surfaced
Union Pacific 4.4 — Strong Found Liisa Lawson Stark (VP Public Affairs) and flagged that the 2026 Community Ties grant cycle was open right now — timing scored 5/5. Correctly identified UP as the best current target.
First National Bank of Omaha 3.8 — Moderate No literal "VP of Community Relations" exists at FNBO — so the tool found the real philanthropy owner, Christina Kahler (Managing Director, Marketing & Philanthropy), instead of forcing a false match.
Kiewit Corporation 3.6 — Moderate Named Bob Kula (VP Corporate Communication) as the closest contact and flagged honestly that he isn't the Community Relations owner and the exact owner is unclear — rather than inflating the match.

A research tool that rates every prospect "strong" is useless. This one gave a defensible reason for every score. In August 2026 I rebuilt it as a deployed web app for the Jet Award Foundation in Omaha and ran 14 real sponsor briefs through it. That showed me what small nonprofits actually need: not one brief at a time, but a full, ranked list they can trust. That became ProspectPulse Reports.
